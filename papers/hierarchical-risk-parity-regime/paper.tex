\begin{abstract}
Hierarchical risk parity earns its keep at large universe size and not below
it. We test it with the population covariance known by construction, so
realized risk is exact rather than estimated. At five thousand names with two
years of weekly data it is the best method we can run, beating
inverse-variance weighting in 90 percent of draws and every optimizer that
remains feasible at that rank in all of them. At five hundred names on the
same history it loses to inverse variance in 83 percent. The crossover sits
near two thousand, so the deciding variable is the number of assets and not
the ratio of observations to them. A study run at the sizes convenient for
simulation therefore reaches the opposite conclusion from one run at the size
a large-universe manager cares about. Below that scale the method has no
interval of advantage: it is beaten by inverse variance where nothing can be
inverted, by any regularised optimizer where something can, in 78 percent of
five hundred randomly drawn dependence structures, and by long-only minimum
variance on the raw sample covariance in 76 percent, so the no-short-sale
constraint alone closes the gap. We also show that no shrinkage class small
enough to be interpretable can reproduce the rule, by a counting argument
confirmed at three universe sizes, and that nested clustered optimization on
the same clusters tracks the shrinkage frontier while hierarchical risk
parity does not, which locates the cost in the inverse-variance split rather
than in the hierarchy.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The case for hierarchical risk parity \parencite{lopezdeprado2016} rests on a comparison. The method is
set against mean-variance optimization on a raw sample covariance, it wins,
and the win is attributed to the allocation rule. Two things make that
comparison hard to read. The rule and the covariance estimate are varied
together, and the benchmark is an optimizer at its worst.

This paper fixes both and asks whether an interval of sample size survives in
which the hierarchical rule is the right choice. The population covariance is
known by construction throughout, so realized risk is $w^\top \Sigma w$
exactly rather than a backtest estimate, and every method sees the identical
sample on every draw. Where an optimizer appears it is long-only, so a
comparison against a long-only heuristic does not measure the constraint.

The answer depends on a variable the literature does not usually vary. At the
universe sizes where such a study is normally run, tens to hundreds of names,
no such interval exists and the window closes from both sides. At five
thousand names it opens, and the method becomes the best thing available.
\Cref{sec:huge} reports that case first, because it bounds everything after
it.

\section{Five thousand assets and two years of weekly data}
\label{sec:huge}

Everything above runs at forty to a hundred assets, which is where a
simulation with a known population covariance is cheap. The regime a
large-universe manager faces is the opposite: five thousand names and about a
hundred weekly observations, a ratio of $0.021$, fifty times more
rank-deficient than the most extreme case considered so far.

At that size most of the comparison set does not exist. Nothing can be
inverted whole, so the only optimizer available is one that inverts inside
clusters small enough to be determined and combines them. The market is a
factor model with fifty group factors, which lets realized risk be evaluated
exactly without ever forming the covariance.

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{rrrr}
\toprule
assets & $T/n$ & inverse variance beats HRP & 95\% interval \\
\midrule
500  & 0.208 & 83\% & [66\%, 93\%] \\
2000 & 0.052 & 47\% & [30\%, 64\%] \\
5000 & 0.021 & 10\% & [3\%, 26\%] \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Two years of weekly data throughout, thirty draws at each size.}
\label{tab:huge}
\end{table}

The ordering reverses. At five thousand assets the hierarchical rule realizes
$0.00487$ against $0.00504$ for inverse variance, $0.00549$ for the clustered
optimizer and $0.00563$ for equal weight, winning every draw against the last
two. It is the best method tested, and this is the first setting in this paper
where that is true.

The variable that moves is the number of assets rather than the ratio. At
$T/n = 0.1$ with forty assets the rule loses; at $T/n = 0.021$ with five
thousand it wins, and the crossover sits near two thousand. A plausible
mechanism is that inverse variance is exposed to the worst of $n$ independent
variance estimates, which degrades as $n$ grows at fixed $T$, while the top
levels of the bisection consume aggregates over thousands of names that are
well determined from a hundred observations. We have not isolated that
mechanism and state it as a conjecture.

This bounds every other result here. The conclusions below hold at the sizes
at which they were measured, tens to hundreds of assets, and reverse by the
time a universe reaches a few thousand.

\section{No parsimonious shrinkage reproduces the rule}

A natural way to read a heuristic allocator is as an optimizer fed a modified
covariance. That reading is always available and carries no information.

\begin{proposition}[Every portfolio is a minimum-variance portfolio]
\label{prop:implied}
Let $w \in \R^n$ satisfy $\one^\top w = 1$. Put $u = w/\|w\|$,
$Q = I - uu^\top$, and $\Sigma' = \one\one^\top + \varepsilon\,QMQ^\top$ for
any $\varepsilon > 0$ and symmetric positive definite $M$. Then
$\Sigma' \succ 0$ and $\Sigma' w = \one$, so $w$ is the global
minimum-variance portfolio of $\Sigma'$.
\end{proposition}

Long-short portfolios are included, and the construction asks nothing of $w$
beyond a unit budget. The question with content is therefore not whether an
implied covariance exists but whether one exists in a class small enough to
mean something.

Class size settles that before any data is seen. A family with $k$ parameters
traces at most a $k$-dimensional set of weight vectors at a fixed sample
covariance, and the weight vector has $n-1$ free coordinates, so for
$k < n-1$ the family generically misses. \Cref{tab:cliff} confirms the
threshold empirically, fitting a taper $S \circ (1 + B\theta)$ for a random
orthonormal basis $B$ of $k$ directions, with $\theta$ refitted for every
market.

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{rrrrr}
\toprule
assets & last $k$ that misses & its $L^1$ & first $k$ that fits & its $L^1$ \\
\midrule
16 & 15 & $1.2\times 10^{-2}$ & 16 & $3.1\times 10^{-10}$ \\
24 & 22 & $9.6\times 10^{-3}$ & 23 & $2.0\times 10^{-7}$ \\
40 & 38 & $1.1\times 10^{-2}$ & 39 & $1.9\times 10^{-4}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{The cliff sits at $n-1$ and tracks it, dropping four to eight orders
of magnitude in one step.}
\label{tab:cliff}
\end{table}

So the failure of any particular small family is a counting fact rather than
a failure of search. Among named families the closest is near-diagonal: a
two-parameter taper that keeps about half the correlation between assets
pairing at the finest split and none above it, leaving a fifth of the
portfolio misplaced. Shrinkage toward constant correlation, which is the best
estimator in that comparison, is the furthest from the rule.

A control confirms the miss is informative rather than generic. Fitted to a
portfolio that genuinely is minimum variance on a diagonally shrunk
covariance, the diagonal family recovers it exactly; fitted to the
hierarchical rule it misses by 0.22.

\Cref{tab:robust} reports five hundred randomly drawn structures. The family,
the number and sizes of clusters, correlation levels, extra common factors,
volatility dispersion, the universe size, the sample ratio and the tail
weight are all resampled per draw. The shrinkage is a single fixed setting,
never tuned.

The third row is the one to dwell on. An optimizer whose only regularisation
is the long-only constraint, which is shrinkage in the sense of
\textcite{jagannathanma2003}, beats the hierarchical rule three times in four.

The advantage holds in every structural family and is strongest where the
method's own assumption is true: 89 percent on blocks with factors, 88
percent on blocks, 83 percent on a nested hierarchy, 81 on equicorrelation,
71 on a pure factor market and 60 on an unstructured one.

The method has a genuine regime, and it is narrower than advertised. At a
tenth of an observation per asset it edges the taper by three percent and
nested clustered optimization by seventeen, because that method still has to
invert its cluster blocks and cannot.

The last column withdraws it. In the same corner, inverse-variance weighting

four lines of code. So at the point of maximum rank deficiency the hierarchy
earns nothing over the crudest construction that shares its one structural
advantage, and by $T/n = 0.3$ inverse variance has fallen behind while
everything else has passed.

\section{The objection: what if the market really is hierarchical}

The evidence above aggregates six structural families, and the fair objection
is that the method assumes a hierarchy, so the test that matters is a market
which has one. \Cref{tab:nested} is that test, on the cleanest possible case:
a dyadic nesting of sixty-four assets whose correlation rises at every one of
four levels, so the structure the recursion looks for is exactly what is
there. One column additionally hands the taper the \emph{true} eight-way
grouping rather than an estimated one.

The objection does not rescue the method. It sharpens the finding. Past a
quarter of an observation per asset the rule is beaten in essentially every
draw, and by a factor of two rather than a margin: at ten observations per
asset it realizes 0.1350 against 0.0671 for the best rival. Below that, the
inverting methods do lose, and inverse-variance weighting wins instead, at 85
percent of draws.

The column that does not move is the telling one. Across a hundredfold
increase in sample size the rule improves from 0.1778 to 0.1350, twenty-four
percent, while its rivals improve from 0.1538 to 0.0671, fifty-six percent. A
method that barely learns from data cannot be recovering a hierarchy from it,
whatever hierarchy is present.

\section{The seriation works; the split rule cannot use it}

The method has two ideas, and a critique has to say which one it lands on.
Quasi-diagonalization reorders assets so that similar ones sit adjacent, by
hierarchical clustering. Recursive bisection then splits capital down that
order by inverse variance. The first is the more original contribution and the
evidence so far has not separated them.

Holding each half fixed and varying the other separates them cleanly. On the
nested market the true order is known, so the ordering the linkage recovers,
a random ordering and the truth can all be compared, and the same clustering
can be handed to a different consumer.

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{rrr}
\toprule

\midrule
0.25 & 1.018 & 0.896 \\

1.00 & 1.000 & 0.947 \\
2.00 & 0.991 & 0.949 \\
5.00 & 0.991 & 0.952 \\
10.0 & 0.993 & 0.958 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Ratio of realized variance to the same construction on a random
ordering or a random grouping. Below one is an improvement.}
\label{tab:seriation}
\end{table}

Inside the recursion the ordering is worth nothing. Hierarchical risk parity
on its own seriation is within two percent of the same rule on a \emph{random}
permutation at every sample size, and at light sampling it is slightly worse.
Handing it the true order does not help either.

Handed to a taper, the identical clustering is worth four to ten percent, and
the true grouping is better still. So the ordering carries real information
about the market and the recursion cannot spend it.

That is the sharpest form of this paper's claim. The seriation is not the
problem and deserves its reputation. The inverse-variance split is what makes
the information inert, because the variance of an inverse-variance
sub-portfolio barely depends on how its members were ordered, so a better
ordering changes the budget it produces hardly at all.

\section{The hierarchy is not what fails}

Nested clustered optimization runs the same clustering and solves minimum
variance at both levels. On five blocks at ten observations per asset the
hierarchical rule realizes 0.1661 and it realizes 0.0892. The one-parameter
taper that dominates the first, winning every draw by a factor of about two,
beats the second in 70 percent of draws by six parts in ten thousand.

One sits on the shrinkage frontier and the other does not, from the same tree
and the same estimate. What separates them is the split rule, and the
inverse-variance split is what the hierarchical method spends its structure
on.

\section{Scope}

Two limits bound the reading above, and the second is not a footnote.

The comparisons are simulated. The population covariance has to be known for
realized risk to be exact, which is the property that makes the question
answerable at all, and it is bought by giving up real data.

More importantly, the dominance of a shrunk estimate is a property of a
stationary market. On a regime-switching market, where a crash regime fires
eight percent of the time with an independent dependence structure over a
permuted labelling, Ledoit--Wolf minimum variance is 1.21 times the
hierarchical rule's variance at a tenth coverage and 1.10 at three tenths.
Shrinking toward a single target cannot represent two dependence structures.
The claim that a simple shrinkage rule dominates holds where the covariance
is fixed and reverses where it switches.

\section{Closing}

Hierarchical risk parity earns its keep at large universe size and not below
it. At five thousand names with two years of weekly data it is the best
method we can run. At five hundred, with the same history, it is beaten by
inverse-variance weighting in eighty-three percent of draws, and at tens of
names it is beaten from both directions at once: by inverse variance where
nothing can be inverted, and by any regularised optimizer where something
can, including one whose only regularisation is a constraint most
practitioners impose anyway.

That the deciding variable is the number of assets rather than the ratio of
observations to them is the part we did not expect, and it is the part most
worth pursuing. It also means a study run at the sizes convenient for
simulation will reach the opposite conclusion from one run at the size a
large-universe manager cares about, which may account for some of the
disagreement in the literature.

The counting argument explains why the usual defences are hard to check. No
shrinkage class small enough to be interpretable can reproduce the rule, so
there is no compact estimator-side statement of what it does, and the
comparison has to be made on out-of-sample risk with the truth known. Once it
is, the hierarchy turns out not to be the problem: the same clustering with a
proper solve at both levels tracks the frontier. The cost is in the split.
