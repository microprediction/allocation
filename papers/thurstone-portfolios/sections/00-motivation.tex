\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

We introduce a notion of a portfolio implied contest that scales to hundreds of
thousands of assets.

As an illustration of the utility of this viewpoint we develop \emph{Thurstone
portfolio polishing}.
This novel construction takes an existing allocation on the
simplex, such as a capitalization index or an equal-weight book, and re-tilts it
under a view on dependence.
The vehicle is a race among assets.

Each asset
draws a noisy performance, and its weight is the probability that it comes out
best.
The performances may be correlated.
The race can carry factor structure, or
hierarchical structure in the form of blocks, nests or a tree, and it remains fast
to compute and to invert.

The procedure has two steps. Abilities are backed out of the benchmark by a fast
deterministic inverse, so that the race under a reference dependence, itself
independent, factor or hierarchical, reproduces the benchmark weights. The same
abilities are then re-raced under a richer dependence law, and the new winning
probabilities are the polished weights. Neither step inverts a covariance or solves an optimization
(\Cref{sec:method,sec:computation}).

The inverse from weights to abilities is what makes this practical. It is the
inversion of \textcite{cotton2026inversion}, which covers independent, factor and hierarchical races at a cost
linear in the number of runners. Any reference law in that family can anchor the
construction. The experiments in this paper use only the independent reference and
the one-factor market reference.

When the reference and target laws agree the benchmark is returned unchanged
(\Cref{rem:fixedpoint}). When they differ, two assets that move together compete
with each other, so a near-duplicate splits a position rather than doubling it
(\Cref{lem:redundancy}). The weights are locally Lipschitz in the correlation,
which bounds turnover (\Cref{thm:smooth}). The target law may be any simulation, so
the race responds to lower-tail dependence that no covariance can encode
(\Cref{lem:tail}). The polished weights solve an implied
mean--(convex-regularizer) problem, $w = \nabla G_S(\theta)$, for any performance
law $S$ (\Cref{thm:objective}).

The construction is loosely analogous to Black--Litterman \parencite{black1992},
and could be used in conjunction with it.\footnote{A closer analogy to
Black--Litterman would shift the relative abilities.} Calibration backs abilities out of a
benchmark, as Black--Litterman backs out equilibrium returns, and the tilt then
expresses a view. The view is on how assets move together, with $\phi$ its
confidence. ``These names crash together more than the benchmark assumes'' is a
view of this kind (\Cref{sec:markowitz}).

\Cref{sec:comparison} compares the race with covariance-based allocators. The
race differs from them in two respects: it never inverts the covariance, and it
can respond to tail dependence.

\Cref{sec:choicemodel} reads the race as a model of choice. The race is
Thurstone's model, and capitalization weighting is Luce's: it fixes the ratio of
any two weights whatever else is in the field. The section sets the two against
each other, with the important context that the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM)
supports capitalization weighting on the whole-market universe only.

On the Dow constituents the polished portfolio turns over four to nine times less
than hierarchical risk parity, minimum variance and Schur allocation
(\Cref{sec:experiments}).
