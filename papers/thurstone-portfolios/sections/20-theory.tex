\section{Theoretical properties}
\label{sec:properties}

This section establishes what the tilt guarantees: feasibility, benchmark
reproduction, how it treats redundant and tail-dependent assets, the objective
it implicitly optimizes, and how it relates to Markowitz.
Throughout this section the abilities $a$ are fixed and we view the
weights as the winning-probability map $w(C) := W_{\mathcal N(0,C)}(-a)$ of
\eqref{eq:popmap}.

\begin{remark}[Feasibility]
\label{rem:feasible}
For every positive-definite $C$ and every $a$, the weights lie in the
probability simplex: each realization contributes one unit of weight, divided
evenly among the assets attaining the minimum. (Even division, rather than a
random award among the tied, is also what keeps the estimator low-variance.)
\end{remark}
\noindent Long-only, fully invested portfolios therefore need no constraints,
penalties, or projection onto the simplex.

\begin{remark}[Index reproduction]
\label{rem:fixedpoint}
If the tilt correlation equals the calibration correlation,
$C_{\mathrm{tilt}} = C_{\mathrm{calib}}$, then $w = w^{\mathrm{target}}$, since the
abilities were calibrated to make exactly that race reproduce the target.
\end{remark}
\noindent The tilt is therefore a \emph{controlled perturbation} of the
benchmark: the deviation is driven by $C_{\mathrm{tilt}} - C_{\mathrm{calib}}$
and bounded by the Lipschitz estimate of \Cref{thm:smooth}.

\begin{lemma}[Redundancy consistency]
\label{lem:redundancy}
Replace asset $k$ by two perfect duplicates $k', k''$ (equal ability, unit
variance, mutual correlation $1$, and the same correlation as $k$ with every
other asset). Then, splitting ties evenly,
\begin{equation*}
  w_{k'} + w_{k''} = w_k^{\setminus}, \qquad
  w_{k'} = w_{k''} = \tfrac12 w_k^{\setminus}, \qquad
  w_j = w_j^{\setminus}\ \ (j \neq k', k''),
\end{equation*}
where $w^{\setminus}$ is the allocation in the original field containing the
single asset $k$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Perfectly correlated unit-variance Gaussians with equal mean are almost surely
equal, $X_{k'} = X_{k''}$ a.s., so the field minimum, and hence every other
asset's winning event, is unchanged, while the pair ties exactly when $k$ would
have won. The even split halves that shared probability. This is a boundary case:
mutual correlation $1$ makes the correlation matrix singular, so the statement is
read in the positive-semidefinite limit. Along any approach with mutual
correlation $\uparrow 1$ the pair's combined weight converges to
$w_k^{\setminus}$, and the even split is the limit when the two copies are
exchangeable along the approach.
\end{proof}

At the independent extreme a duplicate is a \emph{fresh} draw, so the pair's
combined weight strictly exceeds $w_k^{\setminus}$ and draws share from every
other asset. As the duplicate's correlation rises from $0$ toward $1$
the pair's share moves \emph{continuously} (\Cref{thm:smooth}) from that
independent value, and at $1$ it reaches the redundancy-coherent
$w_k^{\setminus}$ by the limit above. \Cref{tab:redundancy} shows this for three equal-ability assets.

The monotone descent in \Cref{tab:redundancy} holds more generally.

\begin{proposition}[Diversification monotonicity]
\label{prop:monotone}
In a Gaussian race, raise any of the covariances between members of a group $T$,
holding fixed the variances, the abilities and every other covariance. Then the
weight of every asset outside $T$ weakly increases, and the group's combined weight
$\sum_{i\in T} w_i$ weakly decreases. The members of $T$ need not have equal
abilities, and the assets outside may be correlated with the group.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Fix $j \notin T$. Asset $j$ wins when $D_i = X_i - X_j > 0$ for every $i \neq j$.
The vector $D$ is Gaussian with
$\operatorname{Cov}(D_i, D_k) = \operatorname{Cov}(X_i, X_k)
- \operatorname{Cov}(X_i, X_j) - \operatorname{Cov}(X_k, X_j)
+ \operatorname{Var}(X_j)$. Raising $\operatorname{Cov}(X_i, X_k)$ for
$i, k \in T$ raises the corresponding entries and leaves the means, the variances
and the other entries unchanged.

By Slepian's inequality \parencite{slepian1962} the orthant probability
$\Prob(D_i > 0\ \forall i)$ is non-decreasing in the off-diagonal covariances at
fixed means and variances. So $w_j$ weakly increases for each $j \notin T$, and the
group's combined weight, one minus their sum, weakly decreases. For an
equal-ability group independent of the field with common correlation $\rho$, the
combined weight falls from its independent value at $\rho = 0$ to a single asset's
weight at $\rho = 1$ (\Cref{lem:redundancy} is the case $|T| = 2$, $\rho = 1$).
\end{proof}

\noindent This is the diversification content of the tilt: correlated clusters
are progressively de-weighted as their internal correlation rises, with no
explicit risk term. The result allows unequal abilities and outsiders correlated
with the group. It does not cover correlation changes that also touch the group's
dependence with the complement, or non-Gaussian laws.

\subsection{Tail consistency}
\label{sec:tail}

We fix the sign convention for this section.
The race uses
minimum-performance-wins and its winner is an extreme order statistic, so it
reads one tail of the shocks, the lower one.
The shocks are therefore
\emph{return-signed}: a crash is $\varepsilon_i \to -\infty$, and a cluster that
crashes together is one whose centred performances are small together.

The
winner is then the worst performer.
Pointing the order statistic at the crash tail
lets the race read the crash copula.
In the maximum form \eqref{eq:popmap} the same
convention makes $\eta = -\varepsilon$ a loss and joint crashes its upper tail.

\Cref{prop:monotone} de-weights a cluster by its \emph{full} correlation.
Yet two
clusters with an identical covariance can behave oppositely in the tail: one may
crash together while the other merely co-moves on average.
A symmetric second
moment cannot tell them apart.

If instead the race is driven by the assets'
\emph{downside} dependence, either approximately through their co-lower-partial-moments
(still a second moment) or exactly through a tail-dependent joint law for the
performances, the de-weighting of \Cref{prop:monotone} keys to genuine tail
co-movement rather than to average correlation.
We call this \emph{tail consistency}.

\begin{lemma}[Tail consistency]
\label{lem:tail}
Let $G$ be a subgroup of equal-ability assets, write
$M_G = \min_{i\in G} X_i$ and $Y = \min_{j\notin G} X_j$ with $Y$ independent of
$G$ and $\Prob(M_G = Y) = 0$, so the subgroup's combined weight is
$w_G = \Prob(M_G < Y)$. Then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textup{(i)}] If a change in the subgroup's joint law raises $M_G$ in
  first-order stochastic order while fixing $Y$, then $w_G$ does not increase.
\item[\textup{(ii)}] In the comonotone limit $X_i = Z_G$ for all $i\in G$,
  $M_G = Z_G$, so $w_G$ equals the winning probability of a single competitor with
  performance $Z_G$, independent of $|G|$, and exchangeability splits it equally,
  each member receiving $w_G/|G|$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For (i), $w_G = \Prob(M_G < Y) = \E[\overline F_Y(M_G)]$ with $\overline F_Y$ the
survival function of $Y$, which is non-increasing; a first-order stochastic
increase in $M_G$ therefore cannot increase $\E[\overline F_Y(M_G)]$. For (ii),
comonotonicity gives $M_G = Z_G$, so $\{\text{winner}\in G\} = \{Z_G = \min_k X_k\}$,
the winning event of a single competitor; this does not depend on $|G|$, and
exchangeability within $G$ splits it equally.
\end{proof}

\Cref{lem:tail} is the tail analogue of \Cref{prop:monotone}. The race responds to
not the pairwise tail-dependence coefficient $\lambda_L$, which does not order
copulas, but the whole \emph{distribution of the group minimum} $M_G$, whose lower
tail is fixed by the joint lower-tail copula.

As that lower-tail co-movement deepens, with $M_G$ rising stochastically while the
marginals and even the full correlation are held fixed, the subgroup's combined
weight falls from the head-count value toward the single-competitor value, so its
\emph{tail-effective} cardinality runs from $|G|$ to $1$. For one independent asset
racing a symmetric cluster of the same ability that limit is $\tfrac12$ regardless
of $|G|$.

The \emph{effective number of bets is state-dependent}: in calm states $|G|$ names
may be $|G|$ partial bets, while in a crash they are one. A covariance averages
across states and cannot represent that collapse; an order-statistic race can,
because the winning event is itself a tail event. Whether a given parametric
family deepens $M_G$ monotonically is a family-specific question. For the Clayton
family of \Cref{fig:tail} it does: the family increases in the supermodular order
with its parameter \parencite{weihu2002, mullerscarsini2005}, so the group minimum
rises in stochastic order.

The response is to the law of $M_G$ and not to tail asymmetry as such. For an
exchangeable pair racing an independent outsider, all three with the same
continuous performance marginal, exchanging lower-tail for upper-tail dependence
(the pair's copula for its survival copula) leaves the weights unchanged.

\begin{remark}[Two layers of tail dependence]
\label{rem:twolayers}
Full covariance is blind to the downside asymmetry (\Cref{fig:tail}, grey); the
downside semicovariance captures the asymmetry but is still a second moment,
dominated by moderate losses; the exact object is the law of
$M_G = \min_{i\in G} X_i$, set by the lower-tail copula, which the argmin reads
directly from a tail-dependent simulation and which no covariance summary
represents.

A Gaussian race driven by the downside semicovariance moves because that matrix
differs from the full correlation when the law is asymmetric, not because the
Gaussian sampler carries any tail asymmetry of its own. In the race of
\Cref{fig:tail} the second-moment shadow is a close approximation (orange versus
green), so a downside-covariance estimator suffices in practice, with the
simulation path as the exact limit.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Centring]
\label{rem:centring}
The race must run on \emph{centred} performances. With non-zero means the extreme
order statistic tracks the lowest-drift asset and swamps the tail-dependence
signal; in the tilt the abilities carry the location and the race acts on the
centred residual.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The implied objective}
\label{sec:objective}

The tilt is defined procedurally, by winning probabilities. It is natural to ask
what objective, if any, it optimizes.

Specializing the population map \eqref{eq:popmap} to the Gaussian race, the
\emph{expected-best} potential is
\begin{equation}
  G_C(\theta) = \E\Big[\max_i\,(\theta_i + \eta_i)\Big], \qquad
  \eta \sim \mathcal{N}(0, C),
  \label{eq:surplus}
\end{equation}
with $\theta = -a$ (so a larger $\theta$ denotes a stronger competitor).

\begin{theorem}[Implied objective]
\label{thm:objective}
For $C \succ 0$, $G_C$ is convex and $C^\infty$ in $\theta$, the weights are its gradient,
$w = \nabla G_C(\theta)$, and equivalently $w$ is the unique solution of the
regularized program
\begin{equation}
  w \;=\; \argmaxop_{p \in \Delta}\ \big\{\, \langle \theta, p \rangle
  - \Omega_C(p) \,\big\},
  \label{eq:regularized}
\end{equation}
where $\Omega_C = G_C^{*}$ is a convex regularizer (the Fenchel conjugate of
$G_C$) supported on the simplex $\Delta$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
$G_C$ is an expectation of maxima of functions affine in $\theta$, hence convex; with an
almost-surely unique maximizer the envelope theorem gives
$\partial G_C/\partial\theta_i = \Prob(i = \argmaxop_k(\theta_k + \eta_k)) = w_i$,
i.e.\ $w = \nabla G_C$ (the Williams--Daly--Zachary identity
\parencite{williams1977, mcfadden1981}). Equation~\eqref{eq:regularized} is the
Fenchel--Young duality for the convex $G_C$ \parencite{blondel2020}; softmax and
sparsemax are the special cases where $\Omega$ is negative Shannon entropy,
$\sum_i p_i\log p_i$, or half the squared Euclidean norm \parencite{martins2016}.
\end{proof}

So the portfolio maximizes an ability-weighted exposure $\langle\theta,p\rangle$
minus a convex \emph{diversification penalty} $\Omega_C$ that is determined by the
correlation. It is a mean--(convex-risk) allocator whose feasible set is the
simplex, which is why the solution is long-only and fully invested. This is the
random-utility ``social-surplus'' / perturbed-optimizer construction
\parencite{mcfadden1981, williams1977, berthet2020, papandreou2011}.

The perturbation law indexes a whole \emph{family of dependence-aware entropies}:
$\Omega_S(p)$ is the cost of concentrating choice probability on $p$ under
dependence $S$, the information cost of a rationally inattentive chooser whose logit
foundation is the Gumbel case \parencite{matejka2015, fosgerau2020}.

Gumbel noise yields $\Omega = $ negative Shannon entropy and $w = $ softmax, a
\emph{correlation-blind} (Lucian) allocator, the Gumbel-max representation of the softmax in
the machine-learning literature \parencite{jang2017, maddison2017}; Gaussian noise with
covariance $C$ makes $\Omega_C$ \emph{correlation-aware}, which produces the
redundancy consistency of \Cref{lem:redundancy}; a tail-dependent law makes it
\emph{tail-aware} (\Cref{lem:tail}). Spreading across independent names is rewarded
and spreading across comonotone names is not.

Since $w = \nabla G_C(\theta)$ is the gradient of a convex potential, it is smooth
($G_C$ is a smoothing of the max in the sense of \textcite{nesterov2005}),
consistent with \Cref{thm:smooth}. For general $C$ the regularizer $\Omega_C$ has no
closed form, unlike the entropy/softmax special case, but it exists and is convex,
which is all we use.

\subsection{Temperature and selection by strength}
\label{sec:temperature}

The program \eqref{eq:regularized} carries a free temperature.
Scaling
the strengths by an inverse temperature $\beta > 0$,
\begin{equation}
  w^{\beta} = \nabla G_C(\beta\theta)
  = \argmaxop_{p\in\Delta}\Big\{\langle\theta, p\rangle - \tfrac{1}{\beta}\,\Omega_C(p)\Big\},
  \label{eq:temperature}
\end{equation}
reweights the diversification penalty against the linear reward: $\beta = 1$ is
the calibrated tilt, and larger $\beta$ sharpens selection by strength.

That is not
the same as monotone concentration in the usual portfolio sense.
With
$C$ having $\rho_{12}=0.99$ and asset~3 independent, and $\theta=(0.1,0.1,0)$, the
weights are $(0.272, 0.272, 0.456)$ at $\beta=1$, exactly equal at
$\beta\approx5.52$, and $(0.386, 0.386, 0.227)$ at $\beta=10$, tending to
$(\tfrac12,\tfrac12,0)$: raising $\beta$ from $1$ first makes the allocation more
even before it concentrates on the stronger pair.

\begin{lemma}[Selection by strength, redundancy preserved]
\label{lem:temperature}
For every $\beta > 0$ the map $w^{\beta} = \nabla G_C(\beta\theta)$ is a simplex
allocation, and
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textup{(i)}] it preserves redundancy and tail consistency: equal-strength
  comonotone duplicates split evenly at every $\beta$
  (\Cref{lem:redundancy,lem:tail});
\item[\textup{(ii)}] a strictly sub-maximal competitor ($\theta_i < \max_j\theta_j$)
  has $w^{\beta}_i \to 0$ as $\beta\to\infty$, the race tending to a hard
  $\argmaxop$ on strength;
\item[\textup{(iii)}] as $\beta\to 0$, $w^{\beta}\to\nabla G_C(0)$, the winning
  probabilities of the dependence law alone, the strengths washed out.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Scaling all strengths by $\beta$ leaves equal strengths equal and comonotone
performances comonotone, so the tie arguments of
\Cref{lem:redundancy,lem:tail} are untouched, giving~(i). For~(ii), with
$j^\star = \argmaxop_j \theta_j$, $w^{\beta}_i \le
\Prob\big(\eta_i - \eta_{j^\star} > \beta(\theta_{j^\star} - \theta_i)\big) \to 0$
since $\theta_{j^\star} - \theta_i > 0$. For~(iii), $G_C$ is $C^1$, so
$\nabla G_C(\beta\theta) \to \nabla G_C(0)$ as $\beta\to 0$.
\end{proof}

Temperature addresses the construction's one residual cost.
When a cluster
de-duplicates, the freed weight is shared with the rest of the field, including weak
competitors; by \Cref{lem:temperature}(ii) that leak to sub-maximal names falls to
zero as $\beta$ grows, while the even split among the duplicates is left intact
by~(i).

Sensitivity to the
\emph{correlation} does not worsen with $\beta$: the bound of \Cref{thm:smooth}
is uniform in the strengths, hence in $\beta$, and with a unique strongest
competitor a large $\beta$ makes the weights nearly insensitive to dependence.
Sensitivity to the \emph{strengths} does grow with $\beta$, sharply so near tied
strengths, as the map approaches a hard $\argmaxop$.
The calibrated $\beta = 1$
reproduces the benchmark.

\subsection{Any simulation, and the implied penalty}
\label{rem:anysim}

Nothing in \Cref{thm:objective} uses Gaussianity.
For any centred performance law
$S$ set $G_S(\theta) = \E[\max_i(\theta_i + \eta_i)]$ with $\eta = -\varepsilon$,
$\varepsilon \sim S$, as in \eqref{eq:popmap}.

This is convex
in $\theta$ for any $S$, so the tie-split winning vector is a selected subgradient,
$w \in \partial G_S(\theta) = \argmaxop_{p\in\Delta}\{\langle\theta, p\rangle -
\Omega_S(p)\}$ with $\Omega_S = G_S^{*}$ convex on the simplex; when $S$ is full-rank
continuous this subgradient is the unique gradient $w = \nabla G_S(\theta)$.
The
weights always solve a
mean--(convex regularizer) program; the simulation enters only through the penalty
$\Omega_S$, which carries its full dependence, tails included.

The regularity of the gradient identity depends on the law, in three regimes.
(i) A full-rank \emph{continuous} $S$ makes $G_S$ differentiable with an
almost-surely unique maximizer, so $w = \nabla G_S(\theta)$ and the program has a
unique solution.

(ii) A \emph{singular or tie-prone} $S$ (atoms, deterministic ties, duplicated
coordinates) leaves $G_S$ convex but possibly non-differentiable; the tie-split
weights are then a selected subgradient $w\in\partial G_S(\theta)$, and uniqueness
or smoothness may fail. This is the regime of the exact duplicate in
\Cref{lem:redundancy}.

(iii) A \emph{finite Monte-Carlo} $S$ gives a piecewise-linear empirical $G_{S,M}$
whose weights are step functions of the parameters.

\Cref{lem:redundancy,lem:tail} carry over to regimes~(ii) and~(iii) only where
the duplicated pair, or the group, ties the rest of the field with probability
zero. If an outsider can tie them, even splitting gives the pair two shares of
the tie where the single asset had one, and the other weights change.
Differentiability and the unique optimizer belong to regime~(i).

In this sense the portfolio is optimal for an arbitrary simulation, though
$\Omega_S$ is not in general a \emph{named} risk measure: the Gumbel case
(negative Shannon entropy, softmax) is closed-form, and in general $\Omega_S$,
the Gaussian $\Omega_C$ for general $C$ included, is implicit. Tail consistency
(\Cref{lem:tail}) says that $\Omega_S$ charges for tail
co-movement when $S$ carries it.

As the social-surplus conjugate, $\Omega_S$ is a \emph{generalized entropy}: it
rewards spreading across \emph{independent} options but counts only the
dependence-effective number of bets, so it will not reward spreading across
comonotone names (\Cref{lem:redundancy,lem:tail}).
For the Gaussian race, or any
law whose winning-probability Jacobian is nondegenerate on the simplex tangent
space, it is locally quadratic, its Hessian the \emph{inverse} of that Jacobian
(\Cref{sec:markowitz}), hence a Mahalanobis penalty in the choice geometry.

This
does not extend to every continuous full-rank law.
Independent noises
$\eta_1\sim\tfrac12\,\mathrm{U}[-3,-2]+\tfrac12\,\mathrm{U}[2,3]$ and
$\eta_2\sim\mathrm{U}[-0.1,0.1]$ give $w=(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$ whenever
$|\theta_1-\theta_2|<1.9$, so the Jacobian vanishes on an open set and has no
inverse.

$\Omega_S$ is not a coherent risk measure (neither variance nor CVaR), but the
canonical Fenchel--Young regularizer of the perturbed argmax
\parencite{berthet2020}.

\subsection{Markowitz and the Thurstone inverse}
\label{sec:markowitz}

The implied objective of \Cref{thm:objective} is a reverse-optimization analogue
of Markowitz. The comparison separates three roles: the benchmark, the return or ability vector that
rationalises it, and the risk or diversification geometry used to perturb it.

\paragraph{The Markowitz inverse.}
The long-only mean--variance problem
\[
  w^M = \argmaxop_{p\in\Delta}\big\{\mu^\top p - \tfrac{\gamma}{2}p^\top\Sigma p\big\}
\]
has first-order condition $\mu - \gamma\Sigma w^M - \lambda\mathbf 1 = 0$, that is
\[
  w^M = \gamma^{-1}\Sigma^{-1}(\mu - \lambda\mathbf 1)
\]
when no inequality binds. Expected returns are mapped to weights by the \emph{inverse}
covariance, and that inverse is the source of the method's fragility: a small
eigenvalue of $\Sigma$ becomes a large eigenvalue of $\Sigma^{-1}$, so a small
return component in a near-null direction can produce a large, unstable position.

\paragraph{Reverse optimization.}
Reverse (Black--Litterman / Grinold) optimization turns this around.
If a benchmark
$w^0$ is optimal under a reference covariance $\Sigma_0$, it \emph{implies} the
return vector $\mu = \gamma\Sigma_0 w^0 + \lambda\mathbf 1$: the reverse problem
applies the covariance itself, not its inverse, inferring the linear reward that
would have made $w^0$ optimal under the reference geometry.

Perturbing the
covariance to $\Sigma_\phi = \Sigma_0 + \phi\,\Delta\Sigma$ while holding $\mu$
fixed, and dropping constants and simplex-linear terms, gives the
\emph{benchmark-centered} program
\[
  w^M_\phi = \argminop_{p\in\Delta}\Big\{\tfrac{\gamma}{2}(p-w^0)^\top\Sigma_0(p-w^0)
  + \tfrac{\gamma\phi}{2}\,p^\top\Delta\Sigma\,p\Big\}.
\]
A covariance change poses no new optimization; it perturbs the benchmark.
At
$\phi=0$ the solution is $w^0$, and for small $\phi$ it departs only as the added
penalty forces.

\paragraph{The Thurstone analogue.}
The tilt has the same structure, with the quadratic Markowitz geometry replaced by
the Fenchel geometry of the race.
Calibration finds $\theta$ with
$w^0 = \nabla G_{S_0}(\theta)$, equivalently $\theta = \nabla\Omega_{S_0}(w^0)$ on
the simplex tangent space (up to the additive constant in $\theta$), the analogue
of $\mu = \gamma\Sigma_0 w^0 + \lambda\mathbf 1$.

Holding the score $\theta$ fixed
and tilting the law to $S_\phi$,
\[
  w^T_\phi = \argmaxop_{p\in\Delta}\{\theta^\top p - \Omega_{S_\phi}(p)\}
  = \argminop_{p\in\Delta}\{\Omega_{S_\phi}(p) - \nabla\Omega_{S_0}(w^0)^\top p\}.
\]
Adding and subtracting $\Omega_{S_0}(p)$ writes this as
\[
  w^T_\phi = \argminop_{p\in\Delta}\big\{ D_{\Omega_{S_0}}(p, w^0)
  + [\Omega_{S_\phi}(p) - \Omega_{S_0}(p)] \big\},
\]
with $D_{\Omega_{S_0}}(p,w^0) = \Omega_{S_0}(p) - \Omega_{S_0}(w^0)
- \nabla\Omega_{S_0}(w^0)^\top(p-w^0)$ the Bregman divergence of the reference
regularizer.

Since $G_{S_0}(\theta + c\mathbf 1) = G_{S_0}(\theta) + c$, the domain
of $\Omega_{S_0}$ is the simplex and $\nabla\Omega_{S_0}(w^0)$ is its gradient on
the tangent space $\{v : \mathbf 1^\top v = 0\}$; the additive constant in
$\theta$ drops out of $\nabla\Omega_{S_0}(w^0)^\top(p - w^0)$ because
$\mathbf 1^\top(p - w^0) = 0$.

For a smooth perturbation $\Omega_{S_\phi} = \Omega_{S_0}
+ \phi\,\dot\Omega_{\Delta S} + O(\phi^2)$, so
\[
  w^T_\phi \approx \argminop_{p\in\Delta}\big\{ D_{\Omega_{S_0}}(p, w^0)
  + \phi\,\dot\Omega_{\Delta S}(p) \big\},
\]
the exact analogue of the reverse-Markowitz perturbation under the correspondence
\[
  \tfrac{\gamma}{2}(p-w^0)^\top\Sigma_0(p-w^0) \leftrightarrow D_{\Omega_{S_0}}(p,w^0),
  \qquad
  \tfrac{\gamma}{2}p^\top\Delta\Sigma\,p \leftrightarrow \dot\Omega_{\Delta S}(p).
\]

Markowitz measures displacement from the benchmark by a quadratic form in
$\Sigma_0$; the tilt measures it by the Bregman divergence of the reference race.

\paragraph{Relation to Black--Litterman.}
The construction is loosely analogous to Black--Litterman \parencite{black1992},
and could be used in conjunction with it.
Calibration backs the abilities out of a
benchmark, as Black--Litterman backs equilibrium-implied returns out of the market
portfolio, and the tilt then expresses a view, here on how the assets
\emph{co-move} rather than on what they return, with $\phi$ its confidence
($\phi = 0$ reproduces the benchmark, $\phi = 1$ is full conviction).

A view such as
``these names crash together more than the benchmark assumes'' is a statement
about dependence, including tail dependence, and enters through the target law.
The two can be combined: a Black--Litterman posterior portfolio, carrying the
return views, can serve as the benchmark the tilt anchors to.

The reverse step is nonlinear here. Black--Litterman's implied returns come from a linear first-order
condition ($\mu = \gamma\Sigma_0 w^0$, no inverse), whereas the choice geometry
requires the gradient inverse $(\nabla G_{S_0})^{-1}$, which the
inversion of \textcite{cotton2026inversion} supplies deterministically and in
linear time.
Only the simple \emph{reference} law is inverted; the correlation- or
tail-dependent target enters by forward sampling.

The closest existing device is
Meucci's entropy pooling \parencite{meucci2008}, which tilts a scenario
\emph{distribution} to satisfy flexible views (tails included) and then
re-optimizes.
The race instead tilts the weight vector \emph{directly} on the
simplex, without a second optimization.

\paragraph{The displaced inverse.}
Locally the two are closer.
For $p = w^0 + \delta$,
$D_{\Omega_{S_0}}(w^0+\delta, w^0) = \tfrac12\delta^\top H_0\delta + O(\|\delta\|^3)$
with $H_0 = \nabla^2\Omega_{S_0}(w^0)$.
Convex duality gives
$H_0 = [\nabla^2 G_{S_0}(\theta)]^{-1} = [\nabla_\theta w(\theta)]^{-1}$, the inverse
of the winning-probability Jacobian.
Here and below the Jacobian is taken on the
simplex tangent space $\{v:\mathbf 1^\top v=0\}$, where it is positive definite, the
constant direction $\mathbf 1$ being its kernel (a Moore--Penrose inverse modulo
$\mathbf 1$).

The local penalty is
$\tfrac12\delta^\top[\nabla_\theta w(\theta)]^{-1}\delta$: a Markowitz-like
quadratic with the \emph{inverse choice-sensitivity} in place of $\Sigma$.
The
Markowitz inverse is not replicated but \emph{displaced}, from return space to
choice space: $\Sigma^{-1}$ maps returns to weights, whereas
$[\nabla_\theta w(\theta)]^{-1}$ maps weight displacements to ability costs.
The inversion of \textcite{cotton2026inversion} is the global form of this
inverse, from desired winning
probabilities to abilities under the reference race.

The construction is therefore
inversion-free in the \emph{asset} covariance (it samples from the dependence law
and never forms $\Sigma^{-1}$), yet convex duality still leaves an inverse, now a
bounded, simplex-supported curvature rather than a lever on small covariance
eigenmodes.
Singular dependence merely means the performances are linearly dependent,
duplicated assets being one case, not that the optimizer takes explosive
offsetting positions.

In a sweep driving the reference correlation
toward singularity (condition number into the hundreds) the choice-sensitivity
$\nabla_\theta w$ stayed uniformly scaled, its own condition number near one, and a
Newton calibration step preconditioned by it converged in a handful of iterations.
This is an observation, not a theorem.
Like the softmax Jacobian, $\nabla_\theta w$ degenerates as a weight $w_i \to 0$, so
benchmarks with very small (or floored) weights can degrade the local inverse
geometry near the simplex boundary even when the dependence is well conditioned.

\paragraph{A selection identity.}
For the Gaussian workhorse one identity sits behind this local geometry.
\begin{lemma}[Selection identity]
\label{lem:tweedie}
With $X = \theta + \eta$, $\eta\sim\mathcal N(0,C)$ for $C\succ0$, and
$I = \argmaxop_k X_k$, the winning probability $w_i(\theta) = \Prob_\theta(I=i)$
satisfies the selection form of Tweedie's formula \parencite{efron2011}
\begin{equation*}
  \E[X \mid I=i] = \theta + C\,\nabla_\theta\log w_i(\theta), \qquad
  \frac{\partial w_i}{\partial\theta_j}
  = w_i(\theta)\,\big[C^{-1}(\E[X\mid I=i] - \theta)\big]_j.
\end{equation*}
(For singular $C$ the identity holds with the pseudoinverse for directional
derivatives in $\operatorname{range}(C)$, where ties have probability zero; along
$\ker C$ a perturbation can break an exact tie deterministically and $w$ jumps.)
\end{lemma}
Both lines are the score identity. Differentiating
$w_i(\theta) = \int_{A_i} \varphi_C(x - \theta)\,dx$, where
$A_i = \{x : x_i = \max_k x_k\}$, under the integral sign gives
\[
  \nabla_\theta w_i = \int_{A_i}
  C^{-1}(x - \theta)\,\varphi_C(x - \theta)\,dx
  = w_i\, C^{-1}\big(\E[X \mid I = i] - \theta\big).
\]
The score of the winning probability is the conditional displacement of the latent
performance given that $i$ won; the Jacobian $\nabla_\theta w(\theta)$, whose
inverse is the local penalty curvature, is a matrix of selection sensitivities.
Tweedie's identity, the Markowitz analogy, and the Fenchel dual all describe the
local geometry of the winning-probability map.

\paragraph{Summary.}
Markowitz is expected return minus a quadratic covariance penalty; reverse
Markowitz reads the benchmark as an implied return perturbed by a covariance
change; the tilt is benchmark-implied ability minus a race-induced convex penalty;
and locally that penalty is Markowitz's, with $[\nabla_\theta w(\theta)]^{-1}$
replacing $\Sigma$.

\section{Smoothness and turnover}
\label{sec:smoothness}

In the online (rebalancing) setting the correlation estimate moves slowly from
one period to the next, and we want the portfolio to move slowly with it:
turnover should track \emph{genuine} change in the correlation, not estimation
or sampling noise. We show the weight map is smooth, with a turnover bound, and
that a common-seed Monte-Carlo realisation inherits it.

Fix the abilities $a$ (calibrated once; they drift slowly) and view the weights
as a function of the correlation matrix $C$. Centring performances by $a$, asset
$i$ wins iff $Y = X - a \sim \mathcal{N}(0, C)$ lands in the \emph{fixed}
polyhedral region
\begin{equation}
  A_i = \{\, y : y_i - y_j \le a_j - a_i \ \ \forall j \,\},
  \label{eq:wincone}
\end{equation}
so the weight is
$w_i(C) = \Prob_{Y\sim\mathcal{N}(0,C)}(Y\in A_i) = \int_{A_i}\varphi_C(y)\,dy$,
with $\varphi_C$ the $\mathcal{N}(0,C)$ density.

\begin{theorem}[Smoothness]
\label{thm:smooth}
On the set of positive-definite correlation matrices, $w_i(C)$ is
$C^\infty$ in $C$ (and in $a$). Moreover, if $C_0$ and $C_1$ both have smallest
eigenvalue at least $\lambda > 0$, then
\begin{equation}
  \|w(C_1) - w(C_0)\|_1 \;\le\; \frac{1}{\sqrt2\,\lambda}\,\|C_1 - C_0\|_F,
  \label{eq:lip}
\end{equation}
uniformly in the abilities $a$. The order $1/\lambda$ cannot be improved in
general.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The region $A_i$ in \eqref{eq:wincone} is independent of $C$. On the
positive-definite cone $\det C$ and $C^{-1}$ are real-analytic, so
$(C, y) \mapsto \varphi_C(y)$ is $C^\infty$ in $C$; its $C$-derivatives are
polynomials in $y$ times $\varphi_C(y)$, which are integrable and locally
dominated uniformly on compact sets. Differentiation under the integral sign
then gives $w_i \in C^\infty$.

For the bound, take a symmetric direction $H$ and write $Y = C^{1/2}Z$ with
$Z\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$ and $A = C^{-1/2}HC^{-1/2}$. The derivative of the
Gaussian log-density in direction $H$ is the score
$s_H = \tfrac12\big(Z^\top A Z - \operatorname{tr}A\big)$, so
$Dw_i(C)[H] = \E\big[\mathbf 1[Y\in A_i]\,s_H\big]$. The regions $A_i$ partition
the sample space up to null ties, hence
\[
  \sum_i \big|Dw_i(C)[H]\big| \;\le\; \E|s_H| \;\le\; \sqrt{\E\, s_H^2}
  \;=\; \frac{\|A\|_F}{\sqrt2} \;\le\; \frac{\|H\|_F}{\sqrt2\,\lambda_{\min}(C)} .
\]
The smallest eigenvalue is concave in $C$, so it stays at least $\lambda$ along
the segment from $C_0$ to $C_1$, and integrating along it gives \eqref{eq:lip}.
None of this depends on $a$. For the order, take three equal-ability assets. Then
$w_1 = \tfrac14 + \tfrac1{2\pi}\arcsin\!\big((1-\rho_{12}-\rho_{13}+\rho_{23})
/(2\sqrt{(1-\rho_{12})(1-\rho_{13})})\big)$, and at an equicorrelation $\rho$,
$\partial w_1/\partial\rho_{23} = 1/\big(2\pi\sqrt3\,(1-\rho)\big)$, while
$\lambda_{\min}(C) = 1-\rho$.
\end{proof}

The Lipschitz constant grows like $1/\lambda$ as $C$ approaches singularity,
because near-degenerate correlation concentrates the density on the tie
boundaries and makes the weights sensitive. This motivates keeping $C$
well-conditioned and regenerating the path ensemble when its effective sample
size collapses.

\paragraph{Finite paths.}
\Cref{thm:smooth} is a statement about the population map $w(C)$.
The realized
common-seed estimator $\widehat w_M(C)$ is piecewise constant in $C$.
It jumps when
a simulated path crosses a tie boundary, so it is not literally Lipschitz.
With
common random numbers, $\widehat w_M(C+\Delta C) - \widehat w_M(C)$ estimates the
population difference $w(C+\Delta C) - w(C)$ with no independent-resampling floor:
its bias is zero given the shared transport, and its variability comes from paths
crossing decision boundaries.

Under a bounded tie-boundary density a path changes winner with probability
$O(\|\Delta C\|_F)$, and each such path moves the weights by $O(1/M)$. The expected
realized turnover is therefore
\[
  \E\,\|\widehat w_M(C+\Delta C) - \widehat w_M(C)\|_1 = O\big(\|\Delta C\|_F\big),
\]
uniformly in $M$, the order of the population motion in \eqref{eq:lip}.

Each weight change fluctuates about its mean with standard deviation
$O(\sqrt{\|\Delta C\|_F/M})$, small relative to the mean when
$M\|\Delta C\|_F \gg 1$, the regime of \Cref{tab:turnover}. Common seeds therefore
reduce the difference noise; they do not make the finite-$M$ map Lipschitz.

\paragraph{Rank deficiency and conditioning.}
The square root is not the inversion it replaces, and the difference keeps
the method well posed where minimum variance is not.
Inversion amplifies small
eigenvalues ($\lambda^{-1}\to\infty$), so a rank-deficient $\Sigma$ makes
$\Sigma^{-1}$ undefined; the root attenuates them ($\sqrt{\lambda}\to 0$), so a
rank-$r$ correlation merely yields draws supported on an $r$-dimensional subspace,
a valid degenerate race whose winning frequencies still lie on the simplex.

Moreover we only ever \emph{multiply} by $C^{1/2}$, never solve with it, and
$\operatorname{cond}(C^{1/2}) = \sqrt{\operatorname{cond}(C)}$, so ill-conditioning
does not propagate.
What degrades near degeneracy is thus not the allocation but
the turnover bound, whose constant grows like $1/\lambda$.
(The symmetric root
$C^{1/2}$ is smooth on the whole positive-definite cone, repeated eigenvalues
included; it loses regularity only as an eigenvalue reaches zero, where it is
H\"older-$\tfrac12$.)

The factor route of
\Cref{sec:computation} removes even this: with
$C = BB^\top + D$, $D = \operatorname{diag}(\psi)$, the idiosyncratic floor bounds
$\lambda_{\min}\ge\min_i\psi_i$, regularizing both the root and the Lipschitz
constant while never forming a dense $C^{1/2}$.\footnote{Rank deficiency is favourable for the estimate: it lowers the effective dimension of the
integrand, the regime in which quasi-Monte-Carlo excels. The eigenvalue (PCA) root
loads variance onto the leading components, so a randomized Sobol' sequence with
its best-equidistributed coordinates assigned to the factor directions (the PCA
construction familiar from quasi-Monte-Carlo pricing) integrates the race
accurately with far fewer paths.}

\paragraph{Common-seed transport.}
We realise $w(C)$ by Monte Carlo with a \emph{fixed} set of standard-normal
seeds $Z_1, \dots, Z_M$ and the symmetric square root $S(C) = C^{1/2}$ (itself
$C^\infty$ on the positive-definite cone): the paths are
$\widehat{Y}_m(C) = S(C)\,Z_m$ and
$\widehat{w}_i(C) = M^{-1}\sum_m \mathbf{1}[\widehat{Y}_m(C) \in A_i]$.
Each
indicator is piecewise constant in $C$, flipping only when a path crosses a face
of $A_i$.

A step $\Delta C$ thus moves the realized weights by two contributions:
the systematic response $O(\|\Delta C\|_F)$ inherited from \Cref{thm:smooth}, and a
fluctuation from the $O(M\,\|\Delta C\|_F)$ boundary crossings, of standard
deviation $O(\sqrt{\|\Delta C\|_F/M})$.
Common seeds keep the second
small: when $\Delta C = 0$ the same seeds give the same winners and
$\Delta\widehat{w} = 0$ \emph{exactly}, whereas fresh resampling carries an
$O(1/\sqrt{M})$ floor whether or not $C$ moved.

The sampling offset cancels in
differences, leaving only the (smaller) crossing term, which shrinks with the path
budget $M$; this is the mechanism behind the incremental (online) update.
The
realized turnover is therefore not zero at finite $M$.
Its sampling component is
far below fresh resampling and vanishes with the path budget, leaving the
population turnover $\|w(C+\Delta C) - w(C)\|_1$.

Quasi-Monte-Carlo can sharpen this difference as well as the level of each
$\widehat{w}(C)$: a moved-boundary difference is itself an integral. For a two-asset
race shared midpoint points give a difference error of at most $1/M$, against a
standard deviation of order $\sqrt{\delta/M}$ for shared iid draws, with $\delta$ the
population change.
For many assets and complicated moving boundaries the gain is
not guaranteed.
