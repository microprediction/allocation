\section{Turnover: an empirical illustration}
\label{sec:experiments}

Two of the properties show in a plain walk-forward: low turnover (\Cref{thm:smooth}) and well-posedness when names outnumber
observations. For turnover we trade each estimator forward over the $28$ Dow
constituents with full 2010--2024 daily history (Yahoo Finance) from a $252$-day
warm-up, updating online each day. Covariances are estimated from log returns and
the portfolio earns simple returns. Turnover is the mean one-way fraction of the
book traded per day, counting the trades that restore the targets after prices
move, and the net Sharpe charges $10$ basis points on every unit bought or sold,
that is $20$ basis points per unit of reported turnover.

Every method sees the same covariance, an exponentially weighted estimate with a
$60$-day half-life updated daily. The Thurstone tilt anchors to the equal-weight
benchmark under the one-factor market reference, with full conviction $\phi = 1$,
unit temperature $\beta = 1$, the Gaussian sampler, and $M = 2^{16}$ common-seed
paths. The abilities are calibrated once at the end of the warm-up and held, so
the daily motion is the full-correlation race responding to the moving estimate
and nothing else. Minimum variance is the unconstrained $\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1$
with ten percent shrinkage toward a scaled identity, and may short. Hierarchical
risk parity is \textcite{lopezdeprado2016}, Schur is the $\gamma = 0.5$ member of
\textcite{cotton2024schur}, and risk parity is equal risk contribution
\parencite{maillard2010}.

The tilt turns over four to nine times less than HRP, minimum variance and the
seriation-smoothed Schur. Of its $0.008$, the motion of the targets themselves,
which \Cref{thm:smooth} bounds, is $0.007$; the rest restores the targets after
prices move. Equal weight, inverse variance and risk parity turn over less still.
The tilt's gross Sharpe, $0.91$, is the lowest in the table, and its net Sharpe,
$0.89$, is below that of equal weight, inverse variance and risk parity and above
that of minimum variance, HRP and Schur.

\paragraph{Scale.}
The second practical property is feasibility. Beyond a few hundred names ($n > T$)
the sample covariance is rank-deficient, so the dense \emph{inverse-covariance
formulas} for minimum-variance and maximum-diversification
\parencite{choueifaty2008} ($\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1$, $\Sigma^{-1}\sigma$) are
undefined; the long-only quadratic program
$\min_{p\in\Delta} p^\top\Sigma p$ still has a solution (the simplex constraints can
remove null directions), though it can be non-unique or unstable. Only the
inversion-free methods stay well-behaved. The factor-form
race never inverts and costs $O(Mnk)$, staying well-posed
and fitting in a few seconds to $n = 3000$. The factor minimum-variance comparator
in \Cref{tab:scale} solves $\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1$ for
$\Sigma = BB^\top + \operatorname{diag}(\psi)$ by the Woodbury identity, with
$\psi$ floored at a thousandth of the mean variance so the inverse exists, and
normalises the signed solution to sum one; it scales likewise, and faster.

\Cref{tab:scale} holds the path budget at $M = 2^{16}$ for every $n$, which is
why the fit time is linear in $n$. The win-frequency standard error of a name is
about $\sqrt{w_i/M}$ while the weights themselves are of order $1/n$, so relative
precision degrades like $\sqrt{n/M}$, and holding it fixed means growing $M$ with
$n$.

\section{Forecast combination}
\label{sec:combination}

Because the tilt returns simplex weights from a dependence-adjusted regularizer
without inverting anything (\Cref{thm:objective}), it is naturally a
\emph{forecast combiner}: treat the $K$ base models' forecast errors as the
asset returns of \Cref{alg:tilt} and read off simplex combination weights. In the
Gaussian tilt the errors enter only through their covariance, so their sign is
immaterial. If the race is instead run on performances directly, as in
\Cref{sec:tail}, those must be losses (absolute or squared error, or an sMAPE
contribution), so that the lowest-loss forecaster wins rather than the most
negatively biased one. Forecast
combination has a long empirical record of the ``forecast combination puzzle'':
the simple average is hard to beat with an estimated-optimal combination
\parencite{bates1969,timmermann2006}, because the optimal weights overfit a noisily
estimated error covariance.

We ran a first study on the M4 competition data \parencite{makridakis2020}. The
base pool is six comparable statistical forecasters (automatic ETS, complex
exponential smoothing, two Theta variants, random walk with drift, and a window
average), fitted to $500$ Yearly, $500$ Quarterly and $400$ Monthly series. Each
series' per-horizon errors are scaled by its mean absolute level and pooled across
the fitting series. Bates--Granger uses the pseudoinverse of the sample covariance
of these pooled errors. The tilt is \Cref{alg:tilt} with the inverse-error-variance
portfolio as benchmark, the independent reference, $\phi = 1$ and $M = 2^{14}$
paths, and it estimates the error covariance with the package's default
exponentially weighted estimator (half-life $60$ rows) run over the stacked errors,
so its estimate depends on the order in which the sampled series are stacked. The
two methods therefore differ in their covariance estimator as well as in the
combination rule. Stacking is non-negative least squares on
the scaled forecasts, projected to the simplex. Weights are estimated on $5$,
$10$, $20$, $50$ or $200$ fitting series, drawn $25$ times at random (once for
Monthly at $200$, where that is the whole fitting pool), and scored
by sMAPE on $200$ held-out series (\Cref{fig:combine}). The number of fitting
series is varied because the puzzle is a small-sample effect. The results show a
bias--variance crossover. The variance-optimal Bates--Granger
combination ($w \propto \Sigma_e^{-1}\mathbf 1$) is wild when series are few and
settles only once the error covariance is well estimated; the equal-weight
average is dominated; non-negative least-squares stacking \parencite{breiman1996}
is a strong, stable baseline. Across the swept sample sizes the dependence tilt has the lowest variability of
held-out sMAPE across fitting draws among the fitted combiners (the equal-weight
average, which uses no fitting data, has none), and competitive on level when forecasters are of
comparable quality and the fitting data is scarce (M4 Yearly at five to ten series,
Quarterly at five). Where one method dominates the pool, concentration wins and
stacking takes over, as the redundancy property
(\Cref{lem:redundancy}) predicts. The tilt is a stable small-sample member of the
combination family, not a uniformly better combiner.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

The tilt touches several literatures. We group them by the question each
answers.

\paragraph{Choice and ranking.}
The Thurstonian (probit) model \parencite{thurstone1927} and the Luce
\parencite{luce1959} / Bradley--Terry \parencite{bradleyterry1952} /
Plackett--Luce \parencite{plackett1975} family are the two classical accounts of
probabilistic choice, differing on independence of irrelevant
alternatives; our allocator is the Thurstonian side of that dichotomy applied to
portfolio weights (\Cref{sec:choice,sec:luce}).

\paragraph{The racing inverse.}
Assigning winning probabilities to multi-entrant contests and inverting them to
relative abilities is treated by \textcite{harville1973} and, in the lattice form
we use, by \textcite{cotton2021}; the latter supplies the linear-time
forward/inverse maps and tie handling that drive calibration.

\paragraph{Capitalization weighting and robust portfolios.}
The CAPM \parencite{sharpe1964}, Roll's critique \parencite{roll1977} and its
correlation-aware refinements \parencite{prono2009}, fundamental indexing
\parencite{hsu2006}, covariance shrinkage \parencite{ledoitwolf2004},
tracking-error analysis \parencite{roll1992}, and the Markowitz optimization
baseline \parencite{markowitz1952} are taken up where they bear on the argument
(\Cref{sec:capm,sec:robust}).

\paragraph{Benchmark-relative and tail-aware allocation.}
Black--Litterman \parencite{black1992} and entropy pooling \parencite{meucci2008}
tilt a benchmark by views, the first through a linear inverse of a covariance on
returns, the second by re-weighting scenarios and re-optimising. Scenario CVaR
minimisation \parencite{rockafellar2000} and hierarchical equal risk contribution
\parencite{raffinot2018} reach the tail without inverting a covariance, by a
linear program and by downside bisection. The tilt sits between them. It anchors
like Black--Litterman, takes a dependence view that may be a tail view like
entropy pooling, and reaches the tail like the scenario methods, but through a
deterministic inverse from the benchmark and a forward race, with no program
re-solved and no tree re-cut at each step (\Cref{sec:markowitz,sec:tail}).
Hierarchical risk parity \parencite{lopezdeprado2016}, the Schur-complementary
family \parencite{cotton2024schur}, risk parity \parencite{maillard2010} and
maximum diversification \parencite{choueifaty2008} are the inversion-free and
covariance-based comparators of \Cref{sec:experiments}.

\paragraph{Long-only allocation as regularized regression.}
Long-only mean--variance is a non-negative regression: the tangency weights are the
coefficients of regressing a constant on excess returns \parencite{brittenjones1999},
and the no-short constraint itself acts like covariance shrinkage
\parencite{jagannathanma2003}. The stable, scalable members of this family penalise
that regression: $\ell_1$ for sparse, stable portfolios \parencite{brodie2009},
portfolio-norm constraints \parencite{demiguel2009norms}, and gross-exposure
($\ell_1$) constraints for vast universes \parencite{fan2012}. The dependence tilt is
the \emph{entropic} member: it returns simplex weights under a dependence-adjusted
entropy regularizer (\Cref{thm:objective}) rather than an $\ell_1$/$\ell_2$ penalty,
and obtains them without solving the constrained least-squares program. The same
machinery is a general tool for stable non-negative (simplex) regression: it shares
weight among collinear regressors (\Cref{lem:redundancy}), stays smooth under
streaming updates (\Cref{thm:smooth}), and is well-posed when regressors outnumber
observations. Forecast combination and model stacking \parencite{breiman1996,
timmermann2006}, where stable combinations are known to beat the
least-squares-optimal one, are the natural application; \Cref{sec:combination}
reports a study on M4.

\paragraph{Perturbed optimizers.}
The identity ``weights equal the gradient of an expected maximum'' is the
random-utility social-surplus construction \parencite{mcfadden1981, williams1977}
and, in machine learning, the perturbed-optimizer / differentiable-argmax
framework \parencite{berthet2020}. Gumbel perturbations give the
entropy-regularized softmax; our Gaussian, correlation-bearing perturbation gives
the correlation-aware regularizer of \Cref{thm:objective}.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The dependence tilt is long-only and feasible without optimization and never inverts
a covariance. It reproduces a chosen benchmark by construction, tilts only in
response to correlation, and solves a correlation-aware regularized program
(\Cref{thm:objective}) whose weights vary smoothly enough (\Cref{thm:smooth}) to
bound turnover and allow cheap online updates and gradient-based tuning.

Open questions include the choice of reference correlation $C_{\mathrm{calib}}$
(diagonal versus market) and of $\phi$; the treatment of estimation error in the
abilities, a Thurstonian analogue of covariance shrinkage; and using
$C_{\mathrm{tilt}}$ to express an explicit correlation view rather than the sample
estimate. The temperature of \Cref{lem:temperature} sharpens selection by strength
and so limits the weight that leaks to weak names, at the cost of greater
sensitivity to the strengths, not to the correlation.

The tail-consistency result (\Cref{lem:tail}, \Cref{rem:anysim}) suggests driving
the race with an online \emph{downside} covariance (lower-partial-moment) estimator,
which \Cref{fig:tail} shows recovers almost all of the effect, with the full
tail-dependent simulation reserved for the residual. Two problems remain open:
estimating that downside covariance robustly and online, and measuring whether the
tail-aware tilt pays out of sample on drawdown and expected shortfall rather than
Sharpe.
