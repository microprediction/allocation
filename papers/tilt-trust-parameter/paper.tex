\begin{abstract}
A long-only portfolio and a vector of latent abilities are the same object in
two charts: calibrate abilities so a race reproduces the portfolio, and the
portfolio is recovered by running the race. Re-running it under an estimated
correlation, damped by a parameter, tilts any benchmark without inverting
anything, so the whole dial is available where a covariance matrix is
singular. Evaluated by quadrature rather than simulated, the identity at zero
damping is exact to $10^{-10}$.

Two things govern what the dial is worth, and they are separate. The first is
how much correlation the benchmark already accounts for: the race halves the
variance of equal weight and of inverse variance, and moves a long-only
minimum-variance portfolio by a tenth of a percent, so it is a diversification
operator that is close to a fixed point on what is already diversified. Its
weights reproduce the direction of minimum variance at a correlation of
$-0.996$ with factor loading. The second is whether the estimated correlation
deserves trust: at a tenth of an observation per asset a full tilt costs
nineteen percent and at five observations per asset it saves thirteen, with
the optimum walking monotonically between.

A tail-aware race, which the construction admits and which the tilt is often
motivated by, is consistently worse than the Gaussian one on both variance and
shortfall.
\end{abstract}

\section{A portfolio is a vector of abilities}

Let $w$ be a long-only portfolio, $w_i > 0$ and $\sum_i w_i = 1$. Give asset
$i$ a latent performance
\begin{equation}
  X_i \;=\; a_i + \varepsilon_i, \qquad \varepsilon \sim N(0, R),
  \label{eq:race}
\end{equation}
and say that asset $i$ \emph{wins} when its performance is the smallest of the
field. Smaller ability is stronger, which is the min-wins convention of the
underlying package. Winning probabilities are then a map from abilities to the
interior of the simplex,
\begin{equation}
  \rho_R(a)_i \;=\; \Pr\bigl( X_i < X_j \ \text{ for all } j \neq i \bigr).
  \label{eq:racemap}
\end{equation}

Calibration is the inverse of that map. Given a portfolio $w$ and a
correlation $R$, there is an ability vector with $\rho_R(a) = w$, unique once
it is centred, since \cref{eq:racemap} is invariant to adding a constant to
every $a_i$. So a long-only portfolio and a vector of abilities are the same
object written in two charts. Over random draws from three to sixty assets the
round trip is accurate to about $10^{-9}$, with one qualification stated in
\cref{sec:scope}.

Nothing so far is a portfolio construction. It becomes one when the race is
re-run under a different dependence. Take an estimated correlation $\hat R$,
damp it toward the identity by a parameter $\phi \in [0,1]$, and race the same
abilities again:
\begin{equation}
  R(\phi) \;=\; (1-\phi) I + \phi \hat R,
  \qquad
  w(\phi) \;=\; \rho_{R(\phi)}\bigl( a \bigr),
  \qquad
  \rho_{R(0)}(a) = w .
  \label{eq:tilt}
\end{equation}
The abilities are calibrated at $R(0) = I$, which is the same law the race is
run under at $\phi = 0$, so $w(0)$ returns the benchmark exactly. At $\phi = 1$
the race runs under the full estimate. No matrix is inverted at any point, so
the whole dial is available where a covariance is singular.

\begin{remark}
That $\phi = 0$ must reproduce the benchmark is worth stating as a check rather
than as a property. Calibrating under one law and racing at $\phi = 0$ under
another leaves zero on the dial already tilted, so the curve is measured from
the wrong origin and every number on it is wrong by an amount that does not
announce itself. Four attempts at this construction failed that way before one
did not. Assert the identity to $10^{-10}$ before reading anything else.
\end{remark}

When $\hat R$ is given in $k$-factor form the damping stays inside that family,
because
\begin{equation}
  (1-\phi) I + \phi \bigl( V V^\top + \operatorname{diag}(D) \bigr)
  \;=\;
  \bigl( \sqrt{\phi}\, V \bigr)\bigl( \sqrt{\phi}\, V \bigr)^\top
  + \operatorname{diag}\bigl( (1-\phi)\one + \phi D \bigr),
  \label{eq:blend}
\end{equation}
so every point of the dial is itself an exact $k$-factor race and no
approximation is introduced by the blend.

\paragraph{Evaluating the race.} \Cref{eq:racemap} can be computed two ways and
the difference matters. Quadrature over the field evaluates it directly. A
simulation assigns weight by counting wins, so its resolution tracks paths
\emph{per asset}: at five thousand names and four thousand paths there is less
than one path per asset, most names never win once, and the returned book
differs from its own input by eighty-two percent. Quadrature reproduces the
benchmark at $\phi = 0$ to $10^{-10}$ in a tenth of a second, where the
simulation needs sixty-five thousand paths and 2.6 gigabytes to reach $0.2$.

The two are used for different things here, and we say which throughout.
\Cref{tab:benchmark} and the direction checks are quadrature. The dial sweep in
\cref{tab:dial} is the simulated race at forty assets and sixteen thousand
paths, four hundred paths per asset, measured under common random numbers so
that the residual noise cancels in the pairing. The Student-$t$ race of
\cref{sec:tails} has no quadrature equivalent and is simulated throughout.

\section{What the dial is worth depends on what you hand it}

The tilt is a diversification operator, and the first thing that governs its
value is how much correlation its benchmark already accounts for.
\Cref{tab:benchmark} applies the same race, under the same correlation, to
three portfolios on a one-factor market with heterogeneous variances.

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
benchmark & before & after the race & change \\
\midrule
equal weight & 0.32059 & 0.16882 & $-47.3\%$ \\
inverse variance & 0.12494 & 0.06148 & $-50.8\%$ \\
long-only minimum variance & 0.02705 & 0.02709 & $+0.1\%$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{One race, one correlation, three starting points. The long-only
optimum is solved, not approximated.}
\label{tab:benchmark}
\end{table}

It halves the variance of the two benchmarks that ignore correlation and
leaves the optimum where it is, moving it by a tenth of a percent. That is the
behaviour a correct operator should have: it repairs what needs repairing and
is nearly a fixed point on what does not.

The direction is right for the same reason. Starting from equal weight on a
market with loadings spread from $0.1$ to $0.9$, the raced weights correlate
$-0.996$ with the loading, against $-0.989$ for long-only minimum variance,
and the race captures eighty-four percent of the variance reduction available
between equal weight and the optimum. Assets that move with the field are less
often uniquely the best, so the race discounts them, which is what
diversification asks for and is reached without inverting anything.

This also explains a negative result that is otherwise puzzling. Tilting a
hierarchical portfolio at five thousand assets and two years of weekly data
makes it monotonically worse, by $3.2$ percent at full tilt across ten draws.
At that size the hierarchical rule is the best construction available, so the
race is being asked to improve on the ceiling and every move is away from it.
The operator is not failing there; it has nothing to add.

\section{And on whether the estimate deserves trust}

The benchmark tilted is hierarchical risk parity, chosen because the tilt then
repairs a rule rather than competing with one. The race at $\phi$ is compared
against the same race at $\phi = 0$ on the same seed ensemble, so what remains
is the effect of the dial and not the resolution of the simulation.

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{rrrl}
\toprule
$T/n$ & variance ratio & tilt wins & \\
\midrule
0.10 & 1.19 & 27\% & tilting hurts \\
0.30 & 0.97 to 1.01 & 48 to 63\% & borderline \\
1.00 & 0.98 & 60 to 67\% & modest, replicates \\
2.00 & 0.90 to 0.92 & 82 to 84\% & replicated to $0.25$ s.e. \\
5.00 & 0.87 & 88\% & strongest, one run \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Full tilt against no tilt, paired on the same seed ensemble, forty assets, three seeds. The ratio is the variance at $\phi = 1$ over the variance at $\phi = 0$, so below one is an improvement.}
\label{tab:dial}
\end{table}

The curve is monotone in coverage and that is the result. Coverage is $T/n$
throughout, observations per asset. At $T/n = 0.1$, four observations for forty
assets, the estimated correlation is close to noise: tilting toward it costs
nineteen percent and the correct setting is near zero. At $T/n = 5$ the same
tilt saves thirteen percent of variance and four percent of expected shortfall
in roughly nine draws out of ten.

Expected shortfall follows the same shape rather than a different one. It is
neutral at parity, where the first run's 70 percent fell to 50 on
replication, and reaches 80 to 84 percent once there is real data.

\section{Where the two effects meet}

The same paired test on a stationary Gaussian market is flat: ratios between
0.99 and 1.01 with win rates from 47 to 53 percent at three tenths coverage.
The gains in \cref{tab:dial} come from a market whose covariance switches, a
calm structure plus a crash regime firing eight percent of the time with an
independent dependence structure over a permuted labelling.

We first read that as the mechanism, and it is not. \Cref{tab:benchmark}
supplies the simpler explanation: the benchmark tilted in \cref{tab:dial} is a
hierarchical portfolio, which already discounts correlated names, so on a
stationary market there is little correlation left for the race to act on. The
crash market restores the gap because a single covariance cannot describe two
dependence structures, so a portfolio built from one is mis-specified in a way
the race can see.

The two effects are separable and multiply. The tilt is worth something when
the benchmark ignores correlation, and it is safe to apply when the estimate
deserves trust. A benchmark with nothing left to fix gives nothing at any
coverage, and an untrustworthy estimate costs regardless of how much room the
benchmark has.

\section{The tail-aware race does not earn its keep}\label{sec:tails}

The construction admits a Student-$t$ race, with fat marginals and genuine
tail dependence, and that is often the motivation for preferring a race to a
solve. It does not help.

On the crash market, scored on the same out-of-sample panel, the Gaussian
tilt beats the $t$ tilt at every sample ratio and on both objectives. At three
tenths coverage the Gaussian half-tilt realizes 0.979 of the benchmark's
variance against 1.016 for the $t$, and 0.995 of its shortfall against 1.009.

The market is not the reason. A multivariate $t$ market is elliptical, so
expected shortfall is proportional to standard deviation there and a tail
metric is the variance metric relabelled; the market used here is
non-elliptical by construction, with shortfall varying seventeen percent
relative to standard deviation across portfolios where the elliptical case
varies one percent.

\section{Scope}\label{sec:scope}

Three limits.

The round trip is not exact everywhere. The solver used for the inverse enters
a limit cycle on small, highly concentrated fields, and neither more
iterations, a tighter tolerance nor finer quadrature moves it. A damped
refinement clears the catastrophic cases, and what remains announces itself
rather than returning a wrong answer quietly. On four hundred concentrated
six-asset draws, fifteen warn and none is wrong in silence.

Applying the race to a benchmark that is already optimal is where it does
nothing, and we have measured that only on a one-factor market and on
hierarchical risk parity at five thousand assets. Whether it is a fixed point
on other optima is untested.

The strongest cell has one run behind it. Everything at two observations per
asset and below is replicated across seeds, and the replication was worth
running: it pulled all four shared cells down and eliminated one outright,
though every one of them passed a two-sigma check. The five-observation row
has not had that treatment.

The markets are simulated. The population covariance has to be known for
realized risk to be exact and for the elliptical check to be possible at all.

\section{Closing}

Racing a benchmark under an estimated correlation is a diversification
operator, and a well-behaved one: it halves the variance of a portfolio that
ignores correlation, tracks the direction of minimum variance at a correlation
of $-0.996$, and is nearly a fixed point on an optimum it cannot improve. None
of that requires a matrix inverse, and evaluated by quadrature it is exact.

Two questions decide whether to use it, and they are independent. How much
correlation does the benchmark already account for, which bounds what is
available. And does the estimated correlation deserve trust, which the dial
exists to express: harmful at a tenth of an observation per asset, worth
thirteen percent at five, with the crossover near a third. That second
question has the same shape as the coupling parameter of the
Schur-complementary family \parencite{cotton2024schur}.

One result points away from the obvious extension. The tail-aware race, which
is the natural thing to reach for next and is often the stated motivation for
preferring a race to a solve, is worse than the Gaussian one even on a tail
metric and even on a market with real asymmetric tail dependence.
